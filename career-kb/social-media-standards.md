# Social Media Content Standards

Channel-specific rules for social-media content (including LinkedIn posts).
Inherits the universal branding (`general-standards.md`) and voice
(`communication-rules.md` together with `sound-like-human-standards.md`). Sources:
`Professional_Branding_Standards.pdf` (Group C),
`LinkedIn_Profile_Standards.pdf` ("LinkedIn Content Standards" — same rules, independently confirmed),
`LinkedIn_Post_Checklist.pdf` (same rules, independently confirmed; adds *Post structure* and *Discussion*),
`LinkedIn_Post_Writing_Rules_Human_First.pdf` ([applicant], 2026-08-09 — governs voice for posts and
overrides the earlier structure and discussion rules where they conflict),
[applicant]'s own rework of the "protzen" post (2026-08-09 — source of *Voice reference*, *No transfer
paragraph*, the spoken fragments under *Rhythm* and the shortening rule under *Technical posts*).

## Voice reference (hard)
A post [applicant] has reworked by hand is the tone reference for this channel and outranks everything
written here until these standards are updated from it. Current reference:
`output/LinkedIn_Post_protzen_de.txt` (and `_en`). Read it before drafting; where it and a rule below
disagree, the post wins and the rule gets fixed.

## Priority when rules conflict (hard)
1. Truth and factual accuracy.
2. The real story and the author's voice.
3. Clarity and relevance.
4. Humour and the punchline, where they belong to the source idea.
5. LinkedIn optimisation.

Human first, story second, point third, optimisation last. Never make a post more professional,
more valuable or more data-driven at the cost of the story, the personality or the joke.
The limit is recognisability, not decorum: a reader still has to see an engineer, so this
licenses warmth, irony and self-mockery — not clowning and not a persona she would not use
in front of a client.

## What a post is
A real thought, observation, experiment, conversation, frustration, joke or discovery she decided
to share. Start from what actually happened, was noticed, tried, questioned or changed. A concrete
situation beats an abstract statement. If the source holds an awkward, funny, surprising or
contradictory moment, it stays — that moment is usually the reason the post exists. Never replace
a real story with generic advice because advice looks more valuable.

## Post structure (an option, never a template)
`Hook → Story → Lesson → Practical Takeaway → Question` is one useful shape among several.
- Never manufacture a lesson. If one arises from the story, keep it; otherwise let the observation
  stay an observation.
- One clear insight per post is enough.
- A funny or thoughtful ending can be stronger than a formal takeaway.

## Humour is not decoration (hard)
- If the idea is funny, the finished post is still funny.
- Never remove irony, exaggeration, absurdity, self-mockery or a deliberate joke; polish the wording
  instead.
- Never explain the joke, and never append what it "illustrates". A punchline needs space.

## Do not over-explain
No repeating what the reader just read, no explaining why the story is interesting, no summary after
the post has ended, no narrating what the post is doing.

**No transfer paragraph.** Never lift the story into a general principle — "das überträgt sich weit
über X hinaus", "the lesson generalises", "dieselbe Falle steckt in jedem …". The reader draws the
transfer. It is the most reliable AI tell in a post, and it always sits second to last, so check that
position first.

## Hooks
Open with curiosity; give readers a reason to continue.

## Numbers are optional
- Use a real number when it is part of the story or genuinely strengthens the point.
- Never add figures, percentages or studies to make a post look valuable.
- Never invent recruiter, HR, customer or market statistics (fact rules → `general-standards.md`,
  *Evidence over claims*).
- Where a number is used, it carries what measured it (`general-standards.md`, *Name the outcome*).

## Preserve the author's certainty level
"Ich glaube" stays tentative, "mir ist aufgefallen" never becomes "das beweist". A personal experience
does not become a universal rule. Never invent conversations with recruiters, customers or colleagues,
and never turn an assumption into a reported fact.

## Who reads this (hard)
Developers at every level of experience, beginners included, plus CTOs, CEOs and recruiters. So a post
carries real depth — a usable tip, a genuine experience — and stays understandable for someone who is
not deep in the topic. Both, not either.
- A specialist term earns its place only when the post is about the thing it names, and then the
  sentence around it makes it clear. Never assume shared vocabulary from her own working day.
- Depth comes from the concrete situation and the decision, not from the density of terminology.
- Everyday comparison over internal shorthand where both carry the same point.

## Simplicity and examples
Keep the core message understandable; drop technical detail the story does not need. Show, don't tell:
back a claim with a concrete situation.

## Discussion
Close with a question only when it follows from the story and gives readers something specific to
answer. No automatic "Was denkt ihr?". A natural ending beats a forced engagement hook.

## Rhythm
Short paragraphs for mobile reading, but not every sentence on its own line. Vary sentence length,
paragraph length, openings and punctuation. Read it aloud — if it sounds unnatural spoken, rewrite it.

**Spoken fragments belong to her register here.** Verbless sentences carry a beat: "Reflex: nochmal
laufen lassen.", "Hätte nichts gebracht.", "Cent-Beträge." Use them where the spoken version would
drop the verb, not everywhere — enough to break a uniform rhythm.

## Emojis
Allowed and part of her register on this channel. Sparing, and never inside a sentence that carries a
number or a competence claim.

## Images
- The image carries part of the message; the text complements it instead of describing it.
- If the image is the punchline, do not explain it before the reader gets there.
- The frame for irony may come from the image alone. A widely recognised template or quote in the
  screenshot is enough — the text then adds no explanation.

## Technical posts: concrete, not dry
- Concrete engineering examples instead of abstract claims; name established concepts where they are
  relevant and supported (`communication-rules.md`, *Use the established technical term*).
- Explain a decision or trade-off only when the post is actually about it.
- Never turn a personal story into a technology inventory, and never add technical detail to sound
  more senior.
- **Shortening removes commentary, not detail.** What gets cut is the meta-layer: what the post is
  doing, why it is interesting, what it proves. The concrete mechanism stays — the three unlogged
  write paths, the 9.300 Tokens Präambel, `127.0.0.1:5432`. The detail is what makes her recognisable
  as an engineer; the commentary is what makes a post sound generated.
- For AI work, name the engineering decision or outcome, not the model
  (`general-standards.md`, *AI positioning*).
- The `applications` repository is evidence of how she thinks — deterministic verification instead of
  model judgement, a single fact base, failing loudly rather than inventing. Use the mindset; never
  dump its tool and quality-gate list into an unrelated post.

## Editing
Cut sentences that add nothing and remove repetition — but a joke, a concrete detail or a natural
turn of phrase is not waste merely because it carries no information. Optimisation comes after the
post already works as human communication.

## Final human test
Would a real person write this? Does it sound like telling a colleague what happened? Is the funny
part still funny? Did I explain the joke, add numbers for the appearance of value, turn a story into
a lesson, or make her sound more certain than the evidence allows?
